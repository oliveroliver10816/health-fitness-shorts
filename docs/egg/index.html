01 — The order of operations

Script first, voice second, pictures last

The order matters more than any single step. The voice-over is what decides where every cut lands, so it has to exist before a single image is generated — otherwise you end up speeding clips up to fit, which is exactly the defect measured in the reference video.

  1. Write 8 sentences. One per clip, in body order. Nothing else about the video is decided yet. Section 02 — already written.
  2. Render the voice-over and measure it. Every sentence must be spoken in under 8 seconds. If one overruns, cut words — never speed anything up later. Section 03 — already rendered.
  3. Generate 8 still images. One per clip. Approve each still before spending anything on motion. Section 05.
  4. Generate 8 videos, each using its approved still as the first frame, 8 seconds, 9:16, audio off.
  5. Lay them on a 24 fps timeline at 0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56 s and drop the single voice-over file across the whole thing. Section 06.
  6. Auto-generate captions, add a music bed, export. Sections 07–09.
Why the voice comes before the pictures

The reference video runs ≈22.3 fps of content inside a 30 fps file — the tell-tale signature of clips that were stretched or squeezed to make them fit the narration after the fact. It produces visible judder for no benefit.

Doing it in this order removes the problem entirely: the clips are always exactly 8.000 s, and the script was cut to fit them rather than the other way round. The grid is fixed. The words bend.

⭐ Word count does not predict duration — measured here

The teardown's rule of thumb was “16–20 words”. Rendering this script proved that is only half the story. Clip 6 speaks 20 words in 6.42 s. Clip 3 speaks 16 words in 7.07 s. Four fewer words, two-thirds of a second longer.

The reason is syllables and commas, not words: “Blood carries them first through the liver” is nearly all one-syllable words, while “acid unwinds each folded protein and enzymes” is a queue of three- and four-syllable words, and every comma buys a pause. Clip 3 started life at 18 words and was cut to 16 for exactly this reason — and even at 16 it is still the longest or joint-longest sentence in the script at every rate tested. The longest sentence is what sets the ceiling for the whole video.

So: write to 16–20 words as the first draft, then render and measure, and rewrite anything over 7.5 s. Never accept a word count as proof it fits.

The line that must not move

Sentences 1–7 describe only what happens. Sentence 8 says the body “holds everything it needs to keep repairing and rebuilding itself” — a description of supply, not a promise of a result.

Do not rewrite clip 8 into “builds muscle”, “speeds up recovery” or “burns fat.” That is the sentence the pressure will always land on, because an outcome makes a better ending. It is also the one that turns a physiology video into a health claim.

Which voice

Two routes, and neither needs an account or a payment:

  • Your own voice — record all eight sentences in one continuous take, with a beat of silence between each. One take, not eight files.
  • Microsoft neural TTS (used for the file above) — free, no account, no key. Install with pip install edge-tts.

Voice used here is en-US-AriaNeural. en-US-BrianNeural and en-GB-SoniaNeural were both tested on this same script and both fit; Sonia is the fastest reader of the three, so she leaves the most headroom.

The rate is set by your longest sentence

A slower read sounds calmer and more authoritative, so the temptation is to slow it right down. The ceiling is hard, and it is set by one sentence:

If you want that slower read, shorten clips 3 and 5 first, then slow the voice.

Every sentence begins 0.10 seconds before its clip does. That is not sloppiness — it is the whole trick. The reference video does exactly this on six of its eight sentences, and it is what makes a word carry across a cut, which is what tells the ear that the narration is one continuous thing running underneath the pictures.

⚠ Measure the speech, not the file

Synthesised sentences arrive padded with roughly 0.2 s of silence at the front and 0.9 s at the back. If you place the files by their reported length, every sentence lands about a tenth of a second late — the exact opposite of what you want — and this happened on the first build here before it was caught.

Strip the padding first, then place by the audible start. build-vo.sh above does this automatically and prints the onsets so you can check them: they must read 7.90 / 15.90 / 23.90 / 31.90 / 39.90 / 47.90 / 55.90.

Rebuilding it

One command, assuming edge-tts and ffmpeg are installed:

bash build-vo.sh

# or with a different voice / pace:
VOICE=en-GB-SoniaNeural RATE=-22% bash build-vo.sh

It renders all eight sentences, strips the padding, places each one 0.10 s before its cut, mixes them into a single 64 s track, normalises to −14 LUFS, and prints the per-clip headroom. If any row prints “OVERRUNS ITS CLIP”, cut words from that sentence and run it again.

On the loudness figure

The track lands at −14.9 LUFS rather than exactly −14.0, because a voice-only recording with no compression runs out of true-peak headroom first. That is fine — it is 11.8 LU louder than the reference video, which is the whole point of the fix. To close the last 0.9 LU, put a light compressor on the voice before normalising.

05 — The 16 prompts

Eight stills, eight motions, paste-ready

Each card below is one clip: the sentence it carries, the image prompt that makes the still, and the image-to-video prompt that moves it. Every prompt is complete on its own — the shared look and the ban on text are already written into each one, so there is nothing to remember and nothing to append.

🛑 Three settings, or the video is ruined

1. Audio OFF on every generation. Veo generates sound by default and it will fight the narration. 2. 9:16 natively — never generate 16:9 and crop. 3. 8 seconds exactly, which is Veo 3.1's native maximum, so every clip is one generation with nothing trimmed.

The ban on text is already at the end of all sixteen prompts. It is there because generators render type as garbled pseudo-letters — every word on screen comes from the editor, never from the model.

06 — Assembly

Putting it together

Any editor will do this — the reference video was cut in Clipchamp, which is free and runs in a browser. The only settings that matter are the frame rate and the fact that the voice-over is one object, not eight.

  1. Set the project to 24 fps, 1080 × 1920. Generated clips are 24 fps; a 30 fps timeline forces the editor to invent frames, which is the judder in the reference. Match the timeline to the source and nothing is invented.
  2. Drop the eight clips in order with no gaps and no transitions. They should land at 0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48 and 56 s on their own. Hard cuts only — no dissolves.
  3. Drop vo-egg-64s.mp3 on the audio track at 0:00 as a single object spanning the whole timeline. Never split it at the cuts. This is what lets a word carry across a cut, and it is the difference between one video and eight.
  4. Add the hook. Copy the first second of clip 8 to the very front of the timeline, so the video opens on the glowing body, then hard-cuts to the mouth. The voice-over does not move — it still starts at 0:00 and now plays over the hero frame.
  5. Auto-generate the captions (section 07), then the music bed (section 08), then export (section 09).
⚠ Never speed-adjust a clip to make it fit

If a clip comes back at 7.9 s or 8.1 s instead of 8.000, trim it, do not stretch it. Stretching is what produced the ≈22.3 fps content cadence measured in the reference. A 0.1 s trim is invisible; a speed change is visible in every frame that follows.

On the hook, and what it costs

Adding a one-second hook makes the finished video ~65 s rather than 64, and the eight cuts no longer land on round multiples of eight. That is fine — nothing downstream depends on it. The 8-second grid is a generation constraint, not a delivery one.

If you would rather keep the 64.000 s total, take the second off the end of clip 8 instead of adding it to the front. The last second of that shot is the least eventful in the video.

07 — Captions

Zero prompts, ~97 cards, 94.5% of the runtime

The captions in the reference are auto-generated from the voice-over — which means this entire layer costs nothing to make and nothing to write. Point the editor's auto-caption tool at the narration track and style the result.

SettingValue measured in the reference
Words per card1–3, typically 2
Card durationmedian 0.50 s
Coverage94.5% of the runtime carries a caption — the only blank runs are on the pauses
Colouryellow RGB(248, 252, 3), heavy italic, black outline
Positionmedian 77.5% down the frame — below centre, clear of the platform's own UI

Two words per card at half a second each is fast, and it is deliberate: the constant motion is doing retention work of its own. Set the auto-caption tool to its shortest grouping, not its default sentence grouping.

⚠ Check the captions against the script, once

Auto-captioning transcribes what it hears, so it will occasionally mishear a technical word — “villi” and “amino” are the likely candidates here. Read the eight lines back against section 02 before exporting. It is a one-minute check that prevents a misspelling being burned into the frame permanently.

08 — Music and levels

The bed the reference never had

The reference has no music at all — its silent gaps measure between −51 and −60 dB, which is true digital silence. Three of those gaps run over a second. On a vertical feed, a second of silence is an exit.

Music bed28–30 dB under the voice, duckedfills the seven inter-sentence gaps, which run 0.9–1.7 s each in this build
Bed choiceambient, no melody, no percussion, no buildsanything with a rhythm will fight the cuts, which land every 8 s

The voice-over file is already normalised to −14.9 LUFS on its own. Once a bed is added underneath, re-normalise the full mix, not the voice — otherwise the bed pushes the total over target.

11 — What it costs, what is still open

The numbers, and the two decisions left

Eight clips × 8 s = 64 output seconds. On the Gemini API that is roughly $5.12 on Veo 3.1 Lite, $7.68 on Fast, $25.60 on Standard at 1080p, before retries — and retries are the real cost, so budget for two or three attempts on clips 3 and 8. Start on Fast: slow, smooth anatomical motion is the easiest case there is. Everything else here — the script, the voice-over, the captions — is free.

Still needs your call

1. Flow subscription or Gemini API? Flow bills in credits, the API bills per output second. It changes the cost model completely and it is the only thing standing between this page and a finished video.

2. The still-image generator. The eight image prompts above are written to be model-agnostic, but they need somewhere to run. If it is Veo's own reference-image route, the stills and the motion happen in one place; if it is a separate image model, the stills come first and get approved before anything is spent on motion — which is the cheaper order.
