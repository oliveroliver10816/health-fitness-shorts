Your esophagus carries it down in one smooth wave, and it pools in your stomach.

01 — The five rules this video is built on

Eight shots inside one body, not eight separate pictures

These five rules were bought with a rejected first version of the egg video. Eight good-looking images that had nothing to do with each other read as eight art pieces; the reference video reads as one journey through one person, and that difference is structural, not decorative. Every prompt on this page is written to these five.

The ruleWhat breaks without itHow it is enforced here
1 · Continuity is a mechanismEight shots with no shared body, no shared light, no shared object.Every shot ends on a stated end frame, and the next shot's prompt opens by quoting that exact frame back. The chain is in section 02.
2 · Say it must look real“Photorealistic 3D medical visualisation” is a genre label. It never tells the model the result has to look real.A dedicated REALISM block in all eight prompts — real endoscope, wet uneven tissue, capillaries under the membrane, and an explicit list of what it must not look like.
3 · Name the real anatomyThe model invents plausible-looking tissue that no anatomist would recognise.Every image prompt carries a REAL ANATOMY line naming the structures that must be correct in that specific shot.
4 · Keep the body on screenAbstract macro in a black void. The viewer loses the thread — this could be anything, anywhere.Four of the eight shots are whole-body, in the same close → wide → close → wide rhythm the reference uses.
5 · Write it to the viewer“A muscle fibre draws them in.” Clinical, third person, about nobody.Every sentence names a part of your body, and it opens on the everyday moment. Section 00.
⭐ The rhythm that makes it read as a body

The reference video never stays inside for long. It goes close → wide → close → wide, and every wide shot is the whole human figure. That is what stops a macro shot from becoming abstract: two seconds earlier you saw where in the body you were.

This build copies that rhythm exactly — shots 2, 4, 6 and 8 all show the whole figure, and shots 4 and 6 travel between the two scales inside a single take, so the connection is shown rather than assumed.

How to actually chain them when you generate

  1. Generate still 1 and approve it. It sets the figure, the skin translucency, the dark coffee liquid, the light and the background for everything that follows.
  2. Render clip 1 from that still, then export its last frame as a PNG.
  3. Generate still 2 with that PNG attached as a reference image, alongside image prompt 2. The prompt already describes the same figure and the same end state, so the reference locks the render to it rather than fighting it.
  4. Repeat for all eight. Approve the still, render the clip, export the last frame, feed it forward.
Why the still still matters, even with a last frame in hand

It is tempting to skip the still and feed clip 1's last frame straight in as clip 2's first frame. Do not — you lose the approval step, and any drift compounds silently down the chain.

Generating a fresh still and then animating it means you see and approve every one of the eight frames the video is built on before paying for any motion. The last-frame PNG is there to lock the look, not to replace the still.

⚠ Where the chain is deliberately broken, and why

Two of the seven cuts are scale jumps rather than continuous handoffs: into shot 4 (the stomach lining, out to the whole body) and into shot 8 (a single neuron, out to the whole body). Both sit where the egg video puts them, and both are doing a job — shot 4 re-anchors the viewer at the 24-second retention dip, shot 8 is the payoff. They are still matched: shot 4 opens on the glow in the abdomen, which is exactly where shot 3 just was, and shot 8 matches on the pale blue light coming out of shot 7.

A match cut is continuity; a random new picture is not.

03 — The order of operations

Script first, voice second, pictures last

The order matters more than any single step. The voice-over is what decides where every cut lands, so it has to exist before a single image is generated — otherwise you end up speeding clips up to fit, which is exactly the defect measured in the reference video.

  1. Write 8 sentences. One per clip, in body order. Section 00 — already written.
  2. Render the voice-over and measure it. Every sentence must be spoken in under 8 seconds. If one overruns, cut words — never speed anything up later. Section 05 — already rendered.
  3. Generate 8 still images, in order, chaining each one to the last frame before it. Approve each still before spending anything on motion. Section 07.
  4. Generate 8 videos, each using its approved still as the first frame, 8 seconds, 9:16, audio off.
  5. Lay them on a 24 fps timeline at 0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56 s and drop the single voice-over file across the whole thing. Section 08.
  6. Auto-generate captions, add a music bed, export. Sections 09–11.
Why the voice comes before the pictures

The reference video runs ≈22.3 fps of content inside a 30 fps file — the signature of clips that were stretched or squeezed to fit the narration after the fact. It produces visible judder for no benefit.

Doing it in this order removes the problem entirely: the clips are always exactly 8.000 s, and the script was cut to fit them rather than the other way round. The grid is fixed. The words bend.

⭐ Word count does not predict duration

Clip 6 speaks 18 words in 5.83 s. Clip 8 speaks 15 words in 6.01 s — fewer words, more time on the clock. Syllables and commas drive it, not word count.

Write to 16–20 words as a first draft, then render and measure, and rewrite anything over 7.5 s. Every number in the table above came out of the actual audio file, not an estimate. A comma is worth about a third of a second; a word is worth far more.

The line that must not move

Sentences 1–7 describe only what happens. Sentence 8 says the tired signal never arrives, so your brain stays alert — a description of the mechanism, not a promise. Never write “boosts your metabolism”, “burns fat”, a milligram figure, a cups-per-day number, or anything that reads as advice about sleep. Caffeine is the one subject in this series where a dosage number feels natural to add, and a dosage number is the fastest way to turn a physiology video into medical advice.

⚠ Measure the speech, not the file

Synthesised sentences arrive padded with roughly 0.2 s of silence at the front and 0.9 s at the back. If you place the files by their reported length, every sentence lands about a tenth of a second late — the exact opposite of what you want, and it happened on the first build in this series before it was caught.

Strip the padding first, then place by the audible start. build-vo.sh does this automatically and prints the onsets so you can check them: they must read 7.90 / 15.90 / 23.90 / 31.90 / 39.90 / 47.90 / 55.90. If any row prints “OVERRUNS ITS CLIP”, cut words from that sentence and run it again.

How each prompt is laid out, and why

Every image prompt runs in the same order: SHOT → SCENE → CAMERA → SUBJECT → REAL ANATOMY → LIGHT → CONTINUITY → THE FIGURE → STYLE → REALISM → NO TEXT. Every video prompt runs: START FRAME → CAMERA → four timed beats → LIGHTING → END FRAME → CONSTRAINTS → REALISM IN MOTION → NO TEXT.

Each heading owns one question and answers it once. That matters more than the wording: when two parts of a prompt answer the same question differently — a camera line and a framing line both naming a distance, say — the model picks one and it is usually not the one you meant. That is also why STYLE and REALISM are separate blocks: STYLE owns the look, REALISM owns whether it looks real.

08 — Assembly

Putting it together

Any editor will do this — the reference video was cut in Clipchamp, which is free and runs in a browser. The only settings that matter are the frame rate and the fact that the voice-over is one object, not eight.

  1. Set the project to 24 fps, 1080 × 1920. Generated clips are 24 fps; a 30 fps timeline forces the editor to invent frames, which is the judder in the reference. Match the timeline to the source and nothing is invented.
  2. Drop the eight clips in order with no gaps and no transitions. They should land at 0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48 and 56 s on their own. Hard cuts only — no dissolves. The continuity is in the frames, not in a transition effect; a dissolve would blur the one thing that carries it.
  3. Drop vo-coffee-64s.mp3 on the audio track at 0:00 as a single object spanning the whole timeline. Never split it at the cuts. This is what lets a word carry across a cut, and it is the difference between one video and eight.
  4. Add the hook. Copy the first second of clip 7 — the molecule seated in the receptor, glowing — to the very front of the timeline, then hard-cut to the mug at the lips. The image nobody has seen goes first. The voice-over does not move: it still starts at 0:00 and now plays over the receptor.
  5. Auto-generate the captions (section 09), then the music bed (section 10), then export (section 11).
⚠ Never speed-adjust a clip to make it fit

If a clip comes back at 7.9 s or 8.1 s instead of 8.000, trim it, do not stretch it. Stretching is what produced the ≈22.3 fps content cadence measured in the reference. A 0.1 s trim is invisible; a speed change is visible in every frame that follows.

Trim from the front, not the back — the last frame of each clip is the handoff to the next one, and cutting it is what breaks the chain.

On the hook, and what it costs

Adding a one-second hook makes the finished video ~65 s rather than 64, and the eight cuts no longer land on round multiples of eight. That is fine — nothing downstream depends on it. The 8-second grid is a generation constraint, not a delivery one.

If you would rather keep the 64.000 s total, take the second off the end of clip 8 instead of adding it to the front. The last second of that shot is deliberately motionless, so it is the cheapest second in the video to lose.

10 — Music and levels

The bed the reference never had

The reference has no music at all — its silent gaps measure between −51 and −60 dB, which is true digital silence. Three of those gaps run over a second. On a vertical feed, a second of silence is an exit.

13 — What it costs, what is still open

The numbers, and the two decisions left

Eight clips × 8 s = 64 output seconds. On the Gemini API that is roughly $5.12 on Veo 3.1 Lite, $7.68 on Fast, $25.60 on Standard at 1080p, before retries — and retries are the real cost, so budget for two or three attempts on clips 4, 6 and 7, which carry the hardest camera moves in this video. Start on Fast: slow, smooth anatomical motion is the easiest case there is. Everything else here — the script, the voice-over, the captions — is free.
